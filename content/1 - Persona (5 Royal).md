
This first chapter will flash us back to one of our old classes.

So, when designing UX, you may come up with something called "personas". These personas are fictional characters whose goals and characteristics represent real-world large groups of users.

We create personas so that we can better understand your users' needs, experiences, behaviors and goals, and identify with the user we are creating our product for. They guide our ideation process, de-mystify our tasks and help us achieve the overall goal of creating a good UX.

Sounds great, right?

# The Persona Fours

So, to get into it, there are 4 persona types, or "perspectives" we have to get to know before we can start creating them.

## 1. Goal-Directed Personas
We create Goal-Directed personas to examine the process and workflow that our users would prefer to use to achieve their goals.

Basically, working out what our users would like to do the most to get what they want from our product.

## 2. Role-Based Personas
While also goal-directed, it also focuses on behavior. With this perspective, our personas are massively data-driven and focus on the user's role in the organisation (what?). Sometimes, our designs will need to reflect on what our user does in their organisation or daily lives.  Questions like...
```
- "Where will our product be used?"
- "What is this role's purpose?"
- "What business objectives are required of this role?"
- "Who else is impacted by the duties of this role?"
- "What functions are served by this role?"
```
...can inform us of better product design decisions.

## 3.  Engaging Personas
These ones can take both goal and role-based personas, as well as the more traditional Rounded Personas. These ones are designed so that the designers who utilise these personas can understand and relate to them better, can engage with them better, hence the name.

The main purpose in creating these personas is to make them a bit more "real"<sub>, uncanny as that sounds,</sub> as the designers will be more likely to consider them during design, and drive them to give their best product.

These personas examine the emotions, psychology and backgrounds of the user, making them relevant to the task at hand.

## 4. Fictional Personas
All the other personas come from user research. This one, however, doesn't do any of that. Instead, it just comes from the experience of the UX design team themselves.

This requires the team to come up with assumptions based on their own experience with their user base and previous products to, in a way, try their best to guess what their typical users would look like. These also allow for early involvement with our users in the UX design process. Pretty good for an initial sketch of the user needs based on the common characteristics.

# 1.2 Playing God (Creating Engaging Personas and Scenarios)

We have, in our hands, a handy-dandy four-step guide to creating personas and scenarios, taken from Lene Nielsen's *Interaction Design Foundation* encyclopedia article.

1. Collect Data
	- Collect as much data from the potential users as we can, start to think about who the major users of our product are.
2. Describe Persona
	- To keep inline with the purpose of our personas (developing products and solutions based on the needs of our users), describe them to express understanding and empathy, while keeping it concise. Add a few more realistic details such as name and age to bridge that gap easier.
3. Prepare a Scenario
	- We need to make a scenario to put our dear little persona in. It usually starts with putting them in a scenario, that contains a problem, that our little guy would want to (or, have to) solve. Putting them in the role of a user will also give 'em life.
4. Iterations (Make Ongoing Adjustments, Where Possible)
	- Surprise, it's all SDLC! We should revise the descriptions of our personas regularly, but also practically. Sometimes we can get new info, or aspects can change that force us change, rewrite, add or eliminate personas.

# 1.3 What to do? (Identifying Requirements using Personas)

As described earlier, we made our little puppets and put them in a situation to understand our users, their characteristics, needs and expectations while using our product. This is that part.

We should be able to describe a specific situation that could trigger the use of the built environment visualisation experience we are going to create.

For example, let's say a game developer needs to feature a realistic recreation of the former Court Of Justice in Singapore... for some reason. They'd be wondering how much detail is required in the scene, which is usually influenced by both the design and technical team.

It'd be a good idea for them to put themselves in the shoes of their player and ask questions like "Where will I start and end?", "Can I interact with anything here?" "How much can I explore this place?"
